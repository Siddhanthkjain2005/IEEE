Imagine standing beside a river and noticing something that feels different. The water looks darker than you remember. There is a pale patch near the bank. A photograph captures the moment, and a short note captures your concern. Now imagine being the person who receives that report. What exactly was observed? What was inferred? When was the photograph taken? Was a measurement actually made? Did an AI system suggest the interpretation? Did anyone challenge it? And, after all those questions, what can you responsibly do next?

That gap between noticing something and being able to assess it is the problem we built AquaLens to address.

AquaLens is a citizen-to-reviewer evidence workspace for stream observations and credited historical photograph reviews. It helps a person capture what they actually saw, ask useful questions about uncertainty, retain optional AI suggestions, and hand the entire record to a reviewer without losing the original evidence. Our central idea is simple: a stronger environmental conversation begins with a record that another person can inspect, understand, and question.

We are going to show the entire working journey, including the parts behind the interface. We will move from the opening experience into observation capture, clarification, confirmation, review, evidence inspection, collection insights, geographical context, the historical archive, portable exports, and the cloud deployment. We will also explain the decisions behind the visual design, the AI boundaries, the data model, the testing, and the next development steps. Throughout this demonstration, the distinction between a recorded fact, a human interpretation, and an AI proposal will remain visible.

Those are different kinds of evidence. A real photograph does not make an example note a real field observation, and a retained AI response does not make a review scientifically validated. That distinction is part of the product itself.

The personal workspace is separate. It is where a user can build their own collection in their browser. The presentation route gives us a rich, reproducible way to explain the workflow without pretending we have conducted field visits that never happened. The opening is designed to draw people in, while the rest of the application teaches them how to slow down and look carefully.

The Observe, Question, and Review markers connect the sculpture to the actual workflow. Observe means begin with the source. Question means investigate uncertainty rather than rushing to a verdict. As we continue into the overview, watch how the information becomes more precise. The workspace introduces the idea of one source and every decision. A photograph, note, assessment, confirmation, and review should not drift into unrelated screens with no way to reconnect them. AquaLens treats them as parts of a record with inspectable relationships. The overview is the place to choose that record and begin following it.

The counters summarize what is retained in the active collection. A field observation count is not a count of people reached. A photo review count is not a count of scientific studies. A needs-attention count is a workflow state. Located records refer to supplied coordinates, not approximate positions guessed from a place name. Those distinctions make the numbers useful. We can explain exactly what each count means, instead of using large numbers as decoration.

The search field lets us look for a source, site, or note. The keyboard shortcut gives frequent users a quick route into the same control. Searching narrows what is visible; it does not rewrite or remove records. If a search returns no results, the empty state should tell us that the current query found nothing, rather than suggesting that the underlying collection has disappeared. This is a small interaction, but it affects how confidently a reviewer can navigate a larger collection.

The source filters distinguish the whole workspace, personal field material, and historical material. Historical sources deserve their own interpretation. A photograph published with a place name and a calendar date is valuable, but it is not equivalent to a fresh observation with supplied camera coordinates and an instrument reading. The filter gives us a way to focus the interface without flattening those differences.

A grid can help us refer to a region consistently. The dimensions and file information describe the actual retained media. The source credit and licence make it possible to identify where the photograph came from. Where metadata is unavailable, that absence remains visible.

The full frame matters because cropping can change interpretation. A close crop of pale material may look more decisive than the wider scene, where reflections, nearby structures, and lighting are visible. AquaLens lets us inspect detail, but the original frame remains available. Display choices are separate from the retained source bytes. When we return to the complete view, we can reconsider an interpretation in context.

There are controls for notes and, where applicable, retained AI regions. These are overlays associated with evidence; they are not edits to the photograph. A coarse AI region is not a precise segmentation mask. A human visual note is not automatically an AI finding. A grid is not a geographic coordinate system. The interface lets us place these aids around the source while preserving what each aid actually represents.

If a saved record has the relevant media loaded, I can add a visual note. A pointer user can place a note on the image, and a keyboard user has a way to place it at the center. The note should describe what the person wants a reviewer to inspect. For example, a careful note might refer to a pale area near the bank and say that its cause is unclear. It should not turn a vague visual patch into a named pollutant. The software preserves the note as a human contribution and records the subsequent workflow change.

Adding new evidence to a saved record can reopen the need for review. That is intentional. A previous decision was made about a particular state of the evidence. If we add a new observation or annotation, the interface should not silently imply that the old decision reviewed information that did not exist at that point. History allows us to see that change rather than hiding it behind a single current status.

The other overview views let us ask different questions about the same material. Compare helps us look at two sources. Evidence flow helps us follow the record's stages. Geographic map helps us understand supplied locations and broader context. We are changing the lens through which we inspect evidence; we are not creating four competing versions of the record. The selected source and its provenance remain the anchor.

In the comparison view, the two sides are explicit. I can choose the sources independently and use a wipe or a side-by-side presentation. A keyboard-operable control changes the comparison position. Both sources keep their own date and credit. This is useful for careful looking, but it is not automated change detection. Different lighting, water levels, lenses, viewpoints, seasons, and framing can produce apparent differences that require additional investigation.

That is why we do not attach a pollution-change percentage to a visual comparison. We have not geometrically registered these images, controlled their exposure, or validated a model of ecological change. What we can do is help a person formulate a better question and identify which source prompted it. The best outcome of comparing two photographs may be a request for a repeat photograph from a documented viewpoint, rather than a conclusion about what caused the difference.

The evidence flow view makes the process visible as a sequence of retained contributions. A source can exist before a report is saved. A check can exist without a human answer. A confirmation can exist while a reviewer still needs information. An export can be available without implying that the record is approved. Selecting a stage reveals the information actually associated with it. Animated paths are presentation aids for those relationships, and they can be paused.

The interface succeeds when it makes scrutiny easier.

We have now seen the first layer of the application: an inviting opening, an honest conceptual visual, a clear navigation system, and a workspace that keeps sources distinguishable. Next I am going to move through the notebook in detail, because this is where the promise of better evidence either becomes real or falls apart. A beautiful review screen cannot recover information that the capture process never asked for, and an impressive AI answer cannot replace an observer's own account.

I am opening Field notebook. The notebook is designed around a distinction that sounds obvious but is often lost in data systems: what a person observed is different from what a system later concluded about it. The original note belongs to the observer. Clarifications, machine suggestions, instrument metadata, and reviewer reasons should accumulate around that note without silently replacing it.

At the beginning, we identify the site and the observation time. A site name is a human description of a place. It might be a river, a familiar bridge, or a documented viewing point. The observation time describes when the person says the observation occurred. Neither field automatically supplies a verified geographic position. If precise coordinates are available, they are handled separately with their method and available accuracy information.

The free-text note is where a person describes what they actually saw. A useful note can be short. It can say where the person stood, what part of the stream was visible, and what caught their attention. It does not need scientific language to be valuable. In fact, cautious ordinary language can be more useful than a confident diagnosis that the observer cannot support. The system's job is to help the observer add context, not to reward dramatic wording.

For this explanation, consider a clearly labelled synthetic demonstration sentence: the water looks clear, so it must be safe to drink, but I did not test it. We use a synthetic sentence to demonstrate a reasoning problem, not to create a claim about an actual river. The visible appearance is one part of that sentence. The safety conclusion is another. A responsible assessment should ask about that inference rather than repeating it as established fact.

I would never place that synthetic sentence into an actual citizen record and present it as a genuine visit. In the demonstration collection, authored examples remain labelled. When working with a real historical source, I would describe only what I can see in the image and keep its source date and credit attached. When working with a real field observation, I would use the observer's actual words and actual observation details. The workflow supports all of those distinctions, but it cannot make an invented story true.

The appearance selector gives a simple vocabulary for what the observer reports: clear, brown, green, cloudy, another appearance, or not observed and unsure. These options are descriptions, not water-quality classes. Selecting clear does not mark water as safe. Selecting green does not identify a species. Selecting brown does not identify a chemical pollutant. The option to remain unsure is a valid part of the record.

This matters because many interfaces accidentally make uncertainty feel like a failure. A user sees an empty field and assumes it must be filled. A dashboard shows a percentage and suggests that reaching one hundred percent is always desirable. AquaLens tries to make a different behavior possible: record what is available, identify what is missing, and avoid converting missing information into a guess. A complete-looking false record is less useful than an incomplete record whose limits are visible.

The notebook includes a Field Studio for media. On a larger screen, capture tools and the observation details can sit alongside each other. On a phone, the layout prioritizes a practical sequence and keeps controls within the available width. Someone in the field should not need a desktop-sized display to understand which file they selected, whether a camera is active, or what will happen when they request an assessment.

I am opening the media area. The user can upload supported photographs or use the live camera controls where their browser and device permit it. The application supports up to four media files per observation. The field guidance states a per-file limit of twenty-five megabytes. Supported photograph formats include JPEG, PNG, and WebP, and supported clip formats are constrained by the capture and validation flow. Unsupported or malformed media should produce a clear rejection rather than becoming a mysterious blank card.

When I choose to open the camera, that is an explicit user action. The browser controls the permission request. A denied permission is not evidence that the user did anything wrong, and the upload route remains useful. The interface includes a way to close the camera. Resource handling matters here too: a capture interface should release a stream when it is no longer being used, rather than leave a device active after the user has moved away.

The photo capture action creates a media item for the draft. The short clip control records a bounded clip, with the capture control designed around a ten-second recording. A clip may provide useful visual context, but we should be precise about its analysis. The local usability check inspects its first frame. The implemented visual AI route is for a photograph. We are not claiming that a model watches and interprets every frame of a video in this version.

After a file is added, its card shows the available technical details. The original filename helps the user recognize it. Dimensions describe the image size. File size describes the retained file. The origin distinguishes camera capture, uploaded media, a public reference, or an illustration where applicable. That origin is more than an internal field: it helps prevent a generated or illustrative image from being mistaken for a citizen's photograph of a real scene.

The original bytes are retained locally through the media storage layer. A resized derivative can be prepared for a separately consented AI request, but that derivative does not replace the source file. This gives us two different responsibilities. The original needs to remain available for inspection and transfer where retained. The external request should send the bounded representation that the user has agreed to send. Those responsibilities should not be collapsed into a single upload button.

The local image checks are deliberately modest. They compute basic brightness and edge-detail heuristics using a browser canvas. These can help identify an image that may be too dark or lacking useful detail. They are not a trained photographic quality model, and they are not a water-analysis model. A bright image can still be misleading. A sharp image can still be taken from the wrong viewpoint. The absence of a local warning does not certify the evidence.

I would show the quality details alongside the actual image so the audience sees that the software supports inspection rather than replacing it. A heuristic can draw attention to a possible usability issue. A person still needs to ask whether the frame shows the stream, whether context is visible, and whether the interpretation is supported. This is a recurring design pattern in AquaLens: a machine check creates a question that remains connected to its basis.

The repeat-photo guide is another practical tool. A person can use a previous image as a ghosted visual guide and adjust its opacity. This helps them line up a comparable viewpoint during a later visit. The guide is removable, and the new capture remains its own media item. The overlay does not prove that the camera position, focal length, or scene geometry match. It supports careful repetition without pretending to perform scientific image registration.

A future field protocol could make repeat photography more rigorous by documenting the viewing point, direction, equipment, and environmental conditions. In the current product, the guide provides a useful visual aid and preserves the distinction between the older reference and the new observation. We do not infer a measured change in stream condition simply because two frames appear aligned on the screen.

Now I am moving to the field details. Coordinates can be supplied through a deliberate device-location action or entered manually. Latitude and longitude are validated as coordinates. The record keeps the method, and device accuracy is retained when available. A manual coordinate and a device coordinate should not be presented as if they have identical provenance. Both can be useful, but the reviewer needs to know how the location entered the record.

We do not geocode a river name and silently treat the result as the camera position. A city center can provide regional context, and a river label can help organize records, but neither is an exact observation point. This becomes especially important for the European historical photographs. Their place labels support catalogue navigation. They do not justify inventing GPS coordinates for the camera that took each photograph.

Instrument readings are also separate from appearance. The implemented fields cover pH, temperature, conductivity, and turbidity. A user supplies a value, a unit, an instrument description, and calibration status. The application can check expected units and basic plausibility conditions. Those checks help catch metadata problems, such as an incompatible unit or an impossible sign, without pretending to establish a site's ecological condition.

I am leaving instrument values blank when no instrument was used. That is the correct demonstration behavior. We should not fill a pH field with a plausible-looking number merely because it makes a dashboard more complete. A photograph does not contain a pH measurement. A note about cloudy water does not contain a calibrated turbidity reading. Preserving those absences is one of the strongest ways the product protects the meaning of its data.

Calibration has its own status because an instrument value and a trustworthy instrument value are not automatically the same thing. Even when the user marks calibration as checked, the prototype has recorded their statement. It has not independently inspected the instrument. The reviewer can use that context to decide whether more information is needed. Passing a metadata check is not the same as validating the measurement method or the environmental conclusion.

The notebook also provides contextual questions about banks, habitat, wildlife, and human use. These help connect the observation to a broader One Health perspective while keeping the language grounded in what was observed. Someone might record visible litter, plants, or nearby activity. That does not authorize the application to infer pathogens, disease risk, a species identification, or a causal connection between a nearby activity and the water's appearance.

Adaptive follow-up questions can make the record more useful without presenting every possible question to every user. The important part is that the basis remains understandable. A question should arise from something in the record, an uncertainty, or a missing piece of context. A person can answer, explain that they do not know, or decline an interpretation that does not apply. The system should not pressure them into inventing an answer simply to proceed.

Now we come to the AI panel. The deployed application has Groq configured for both text clarification and photograph candidate findings. The configured model identifier in this deployment is qwen slash qwen three point eight, twenty-seven billion, shown in the technical metadata as qwen/qwen3.8-27b. That identifier describes the selected provider model. It is not a claim that we trained a model ourselves or that the model has been scientifically validated for river assessment.

The text consent control is explicit about the recipient and the material being sent. The user can choose to send the note and selected appearance for clarification. Until they make that choice and request the assessment, simply opening the notebook is not a live AI analysis. The disclosure makes the external step understandable at the point of use, instead of hiding it inside a general-purpose privacy statement somewhere else on the site.

Photo consent is separate. A user may be comfortable asking for help with a sentence while deciding not to send an image. They may also want a visual suggestion while keeping unrelated notes local. The photograph control explains that a resized photograph is sent for candidate findings. These separate choices reflect the different contents and possible sensitivities of text and imagery. They also make the recorded provider context easier to explain later.

If AI is not selected, the deterministic local assessment remains available. That is a working path through the product, not a pretend AI response. If a live text request fails or times out, the interface should preserve the distinction between the attempted request and the rules-based result. It should not label a fallback as a successful model answer. A person should be able to continue collecting and reviewing evidence while understanding which source produced each question.

I am now choosing the action that finds the questions worth asking. The progress state tells us that the evidence is being checked. A successful response leads into clarification. In a live recording, I would read the actual returned questions rather than a scripted prediction of which issues will appear. Models can vary, and the result can be empty or unavailable. The explanation should match the response on the screen, even when that response is less dramatic than expected.

The text AI is constrained to a small number of structured suggestions. It must use allowed issue categories and ground relevant questions in exact spans from the submitted note. The application validates that structure before using it. The goal is to help identify unsupported inferences, ambiguous descriptions, or places where context would help. We do not ask the model to invent measurements, rewrite the observer's history, or issue an environmental verdict.

Exact quote grounding is an important engineering decision. If a suggestion says a particular claim needs clarification, the user should be able to see which words prompted that suggestion. This makes a challenge more intelligible and reduces the risk of the interface attributing a statement to the observer that they never made. The quote is evidence of what was written, not evidence that the statement is true.

On the clarification screen, the language is a question, not a verdict. The interface shows the issue, the relevant original words, and the reason more context would help. The answer area lets the observer append information. The original note stays intact. If the observer explains that they did not perform a test, that explanation becomes part of the record without making the original safety inference disappear from history.

The three response paths are meaningful. Confirm clarification records an answer after the user supplies one. Keep as uncertain explicitly retains uncertainty. This question does not apply records a dismissal. These are different dispositions, and they matter to a reviewer. A dismissed question is not necessarily a resolved scientific issue. An uncertain answer is not a failed submission. A clarification can improve the record even when it confirms that the observer does not know.

I would demonstrate that by selecting an uncertainty path on an appropriate example rather than filling every answer with confident prose. A reviewable record can say that a pale patch was visible and its cause was unknown. That is a legitimate outcome. The application should help preserve a useful question for a future visit or a qualified reviewer, rather than force the person to name the patch before they can save the observation.

The visual AI follows a parallel but separate route. After the user consents and selects a photograph request, the model may return a bounded set of candidate categories with coarse regions and qualitative confidence. The interface keeps the provider, model, and assessment time with the response. Confidence is explicitly uncalibrated. It is a description supplied by the model in this workflow, not a measured probability that an environmental condition is present.

A candidate near the left bank is an invitation to look at that region. It is not a verified object detection with a scientifically measured boundary. A candidate about visible surface material is not a chemical identification. If the model returns no enumerated candidates, we retain that result honestly. An empty result does not mean the water is healthy, and it does not establish that a particular hazard is absent. The image may simply be insufficient, or the model may have missed something.

This is also where we explain the difference between a new live request and a recorded response. Some presentation records retain earlier responses from Gemini. They keep their original provider, model, and time. The deployment's current provider being Groq does not retroactively change who produced an older response. That historical honesty allows a reviewer to understand which system contributed to a particular record, even after configuration changes.

The notebook journey ends with a deliberate confirmation screen. Its message is your observation, your confirmation. We can inspect the site, the time or historical source date, the selected appearance, the original note, and the appended clarifications. We can also review uncertainty, dismissals, media, follow-up answers, and the completeness information. The point is to give the user a meaningful final review of what will be saved.

The confirmation checkbox changes its meaning appropriately for a historical photograph review. In that case, the person confirms their review of a credited source rather than claiming to have visited the river on the source date. For a genuine field observation, the person confirms their submitted record. In both cases, confirmation establishes approval of the record in this workflow. It does not transform the contents into scientific truth.

Save observation for review becomes available after the explicit confirmation. Returning to the original draft requires the confirmation to be reconsidered rather than carrying it silently across edits. That is a small state-management detail with a large implication: approval should refer to the content the person actually saw. Once saved, the report carries the original, the checks, the responses, the field evidence, and the beginning of its decision history into the next part of the application.

We have now moved from an observation to something another person can inspect. The result is more than a polished paragraph generated by AI. It is a structured record that preserves the source and separates contributions. Next, I am opening the Review desk to show how a reviewer can agree, disagree, ask for information, and explain their reasoning without erasing the earlier stages.

The Review desk begins with the instruction to follow the evidence. That phrase captures the reviewer's role. The reviewer is not being asked to accept a machine-generated summary at face value. They are being given a route back to the original material, the questions raised about it, the responses, and the remaining gaps. The product's strongest claim is that those relationships stay inspectable.

Notice the reviewer badge. It identifies a demo reviewer with no separate account. This prototype does not authenticate an independent expert or enforce organizational roles. The person operating the browser can exercise the review workflow. That lets us demonstrate the interaction, but it would be misleading to describe a local review as an authenticated expert endorsement. The interface exposes this limitation instead of relying on a role name to create an impression of authority.

The counts distinguish records awaiting review from reviews already recorded. The search field accepts sites, notes, or record identifiers. The filter separates all reports, awaiting review, requests for more information, and reviewed records. Each combination provides a different view of the saved collection. If the selected filters return nothing, we can clear them or begin a first observation. An empty review list is a legitimate state that the interface handles directly.

I am opening one record. The receipt brings the original evidence and every decision together. At the top, the status describes the current workflow. A historical photo review is labelled as such. A demonstration note explains the example provenance where relevant. The export action belongs to the actual saved record. None of these elements should imply that opening the receipt itself performs a review.

The original section shows what was observed or what was written about the source photograph. The note remains unchanged. The appearance field is attributed to the citizen or photo reviewer. The date is interpreted according to the record type. In a historical review, a source date belongs to the photograph's provenance, while the date when the review was saved belongs to the application's workflow. Combining those dates would create a false account of who was present and when.

The media section lets us inspect the retained image and its associated metadata. If the media bytes are available in this browser, the file can be displayed and the original can be accessed through the available controls. If only a reference remains, the application should not imply that the complete file is present. This distinction is especially relevant after an import that carries report metadata without every original media file.

A file digest helps establish which retained bytes an assessment refers to. AquaLens uses SHA-256 for that identity. If the bytes change, the digest changes. That is useful for associating an AI response with the correct source and for checking a transferred field pack. It does not prove where a photograph was taken, who took it, or whether the scene was staged. A hash is an identity check for bytes, not a certificate of truth.

Now I am inspecting the AI candidates associated with this media. A valid association requires the expected source identity and matching image evidence. We do not take a response from one photograph and display it beside another merely because both depict the same city or river. The visual result remains attached to the media that prompted it. That source matching is important when the archive contains many related photographs.

The candidate carries its category, coarse region, qualitative confidence, provider, model, and assessment time. The reviewer can support it, disagree with it, or mark it uncertain, and must provide a reason. The reason gives the next reader something to inspect. A button saying disagree without an explanation would tell us much less about whether the reviewer found a reflection, an ambiguous shape, or an unsupported leap in interpretation.

Suppose a model proposes visible surface material near a bank, and the reviewer thinks the area may be a reflection. A useful disagreement would describe the visual basis and preserve uncertainty if the image is insufficient. The original candidate stays in the record, alongside the human disposition. We are not deleting the machine's earlier contribution to make the final record look cleaner. Disagreement is information about the assessment process.

Supporting a candidate also has limits. A reviewer may agree that a visible feature appears in the image while remaining unable to identify its cause. Support does not convert an appearance into a chemical analysis. The same applies to uncertainty. A reviewer can say the resolution or lighting is insufficient, and that response remains meaningful. The workflow does not require every proposal to become either a confident acceptance or a confident rejection.

The questions and clarifications section then lets the reviewer see how the observer responded to text questions. The original quote is visible. An appended answer is attributed to the citizen and remains unverified as a factual claim. If no additional factual evidence was supplied, the interface says so. That prevents a workflow completion marker from being mistaken for the resolution of every substantive uncertainty.

The reviewer can record a reason and choose a review action. A request for more information records what would help. Marking a record reviewed records that a person has examined it within this local workflow. Neither action certifies the water. The current status and the history of earlier actions remain distinct, so the latest decision can be understood in context.

A request for more information is currently stored in this browser. It does not automatically send a message to a citizen, notify a laboratory, or create a task in an external organization's system. That is an important operational boundary. The wording may describe the next step, but the implemented communication mechanism is the retained record and its export. Shared accounts and notifications would be a later product layer.

I am moving down to the evidence trail. This is where we can follow creation, confirmation, annotations, and review events that were actually retained. Each event explains what happened in the workflow. The trail is valuable for transparency, but it is not a tamper-proof server ledger. Because this version stores records locally and does not authenticate reviewers, we should describe it as retained history, not cryptographic non-repudiation or institutional audit certification.

Let us trace the architecture from the browser inward. The interface is built with React and TypeScript. React organizes the interactive views into components, while TypeScript helps describe the shapes of observations, media, assessments, and decisions during development. The application uses Next-compatible routing and server APIs through the Vinext build and runtime setup on Vite. That distinction is useful because it describes the actual implementation rather than assuming a particular hosting platform from a familiar framework name.

The verified dependency set includes React nineteen, TypeScript five point nine, Vite eight, and the installed Vinext beta. We retain a lockfile to make dependency resolution reproducible. A beta runtime is an engineering dependency to evaluate, not a reason to claim mature compatibility with every framework feature. The successful build and deployment checks establish the paths we exercised in this application.

The main routes have separate purposes. The root route is the personal workspace. The showcase route opens the labelled presentation collection. The visuals route opens the archive and associated views, with collection context carried where relevant. The server API routes handle bounded assessment requests and capability information. Static assets include fonts, credited images, retained context bundles, generated evaluation reports, and the files supporting the offline experience.

The record model separates the original observation from later contributions. The original holds the site, reported time, appearance, note, and synthetic status where applicable. Assessment data records rule or AI questions and their dispositions. Field evidence holds coordinates, media references, supplied readings, follow-up answers, and visual judgments. Confirmation and review history add the human workflow. This separation makes it possible to render a receipt, a graph, a table, or a narrative without rewriting the underlying source.

Runtime validation is as important as compile-time types. TypeScript can help us while writing code, but it cannot guarantee that a downloaded file or provider response has the expected shape. AquaLens uses Zod schemas to validate data at boundaries. That includes records being restored or imported and constrained AI responses. A malformed object should be rejected or handled explicitly before it can become a trusted-looking interface element.

Cross-field validation adds another layer. A historical source record needs consistent reference identity. Media origins, digests, and source associations must agree. A field value should have the required supporting metadata. An identifier alone is not enough if the associated bytes or record type contradict it. These checks help prevent accidental combinations that would look plausible on screen while carrying the wrong provenance.

State transitions are part of the model too. Draft, clarification, confirmation, saved review state, and later annotations represent different stages. An explicit confirmation should refer to the content the user reviewed. Adding relevant evidence can reopen review. A replay should not mutate saved decisions. An import should not overwrite an existing conflicting record. These are behavioral rules, and the test suite exercises them as more than visual styling choices.

Browser storage is deliberately split. Structured records use browser-local storage with versioned parsing and validation. Media bytes use IndexedDB through the idb library. This is a practical separation because binary media and structured record metadata have different storage needs. The record can describe a media item while the byte store holds the actual file needed to display or export it.

That architecture makes a local-first workflow possible, but we should use the term precisely. Records in one browser are not automatically synchronized to another browser. They can be affected by browser storage policies or user deletion. Hosting the interface in Google Cloud does not create a shared database of observations. Field packs provide deliberate portability in this version. An authenticated shared backend would be a separate future implementation.

The personal and presentation collections use separate namespaces. This prevents a demo record from appearing as if it were a personal field observation simply because the same browser opened both routes. It also allows a presenter to explore and edit examples without contaminating the personal collection. The distinction is carried into labels, filters, export handling, and import safeguards, rather than relying only on a different page title.

SHA-256 digests support byte matching across several features. They help bind retained AI context to the intended image, validate media during transfer, and explain which original file a receipt references. A digest gives us a deterministic identity for the bytes. It does not require us to alter the source image, and it does not confer authenticity on an unknown source. That limited but valuable role is consistent throughout the product.

The UI uses established primitives for dialogs, tabs, and other controls, together with custom components and original styling. Radix supplies important interaction foundations. Lucide supplies icons. Motion supports selected animated experiences. Recharts is used for charting where appropriate, and React Flow supports the relational evidence graph. We chose tools for specific jobs rather than adding a new rendering framework for every visual idea.

Three.js is responsible for the conceptual river scene. MapLibre is responsible for the geographic map. Those renderers have different inputs and lifecycles. The river scene creates its own geometry and materials. The map consumes geographic styles and raster sources. Their shared responsibility is to preserve surrounding controls and explanatory content when graphics are unavailable. The evidence itself should remain accessible even if an optional rendering surface fails.

The frontend rebuild also paid attention to layout containment. A data matrix can be wider than a phone. A filmstrip can contain more cards than fit on screen. A control group can have long labels. The solution is to give those elements intentional local overflow behavior and adaptable composition, rather than let them enlarge the entire document. Small-screen checks found and corrected specific overflow issues in the review and insights areas.

Performance work focused on useful boundaries. The Three.js scene is loaded when needed and limits its rendering effort. Heavy visuals have cleanup behavior. Charts receive sensible dimensions to avoid invalid initial measurements. Source cards are progressively revealed. Media requests are bounded. These are concrete measures we implemented. We are not claiming a universal frame rate, a performance score, or flawless behavior on every device we have not tested.

Now I will trace a text AI request. The browser first obtains public capability information, including whether AI is configured, the selected provider, the configured model, and the consent scope. The consent scope identifies the recipient and model configuration without exposing credentials. If the relevant configuration changes, the earlier consent should not silently authorize a different recipient. That is why capability discovery and consent are connected.

After the user explicitly chooses the text AI path, the browser sends the bounded observation input to the application's server route. The server validates the input and consent context, checks the request boundary, and uses its server-side provider configuration. The browser does not receive the provider key. The provider response is then validated before the application merges accepted suggestions into the assessment flow.

The text model is asked for allowed issue codes and exact contiguous quotes, rather than unrestricted advice. The application supplies fixed cautious question wording for those codes. This reduces the amount of arbitrary generated prose that can enter the workflow. The model helps identify where a question may be useful, while the application controls the categories, structure, and user-facing framing that have been designed for this assessment task.

The allowed text categories include unsupported conclusions, conflicting observations, unavailable evidence, and ambiguity. Those categories are constrained by the instructions. Ordinary color descriptions do not automatically deserve a warning. Different times or locations can explain different appearances. Negated conclusions and explicit unknowns should not be treated as affirmative claims. These details matter because careless automated checking can make a citizen's honest note harder to submit rather than more useful.

The model can return no issues. That is a valid output when no permitted question is needed. The server still validates the response shape and quote grounding. A successful HTTP response alone is not enough; the response must be usable within the application's contract. Unexpected tools, invalid structures, unsupported categories, and incomplete provider outputs do not deserve to become authoritative-looking assessment cards.

The photo request follows a similar server-side boundary with a different input and schema. A resized image derivative is sent after separate consent. The model returns at most five distinct allowed candidate findings, with a coarse region and low, medium, or high qualitative confidence. The application does not accept unrestricted claims about pollutants, species, pathogens, pH, water health, or causes as visual findings in this workflow.

The prompts explicitly treat input text and text appearing inside images as untrusted content. If a note or photograph contains instructions telling the model to ignore its task, those instructions are part of the evidence being assessed, not authority over the application. Structured validation provides another boundary after the model responds. We should describe this as a layered risk-reduction design, not a proof that prompt injection or model error has become impossible.

Request budgets and abort handling keep the application from waiting indefinitely. Text and visual calls have bounded total time budgets, and the provider adapter limits attempts. The interface can show failure or a fallback path while retaining the user's original work. A long-running external service should not hold the observation hostage. The local assessment path preserves a useful workflow when the optional provider is unavailable.

The four configured Groq credentials are handled conservatively. Backup credentials are used only for a rejected credential condition. A rate-limit response is not a reason to rotate through keys to evade a service limit. Multiple keys do not imply multiple independent quotas. The implementation also avoids silently switching a user's data to an unrelated provider. An alternative provider requires explicit configuration and matching consent; the deployed setup uses Groq without a separately configured fallback provider.

Now I want to connect this implementation to impact. The problem we target is an evidence handoff problem. A citizen can notice something important without having the equipment or expertise to interpret every aspect of it. A reviewer can bring expertise but still struggle if the original source, context, and uncertainties have been lost. AquaLens creates a shared structure for that handoff. Its immediate value is a more inspectable record and a clearer next question.

That value can be described through a simple sequence. A person begins with a short account and optional media. The application helps identify where clarification may be useful. The person responds without losing their original words. A reviewer examines the source, challenges proposals, and records a reason. The resulting receipt can be inspected, replayed in limited ways, and exported. Every stage has a visible role, and no single stage is treated as a substitute for all the others.

For a future pilot, we would measure whether this structure actually improves the work. We could compare the amount of missing context before and after clarification, the time required for a reviewer to locate supporting evidence, the proportion of decisions with usable reasons, and the frequency of unsupported conclusions that remain in submitted interpretations. Those are proposed evaluation measures, not results we have already obtained.

We would also measure the costs of the interaction. A system can ask so many questions that people abandon it. We would want to know whether the questions are relevant, whether users understand the consent choices, whether uncertainty feels acceptable, and whether the mobile experience works under realistic field conditions. Better evidence is not achieved merely by increasing the number of required fields. The workflow must remain practical for the person contributing it.

A separate model evaluation would need appropriately selected and reviewed examples. We would distinguish text issue identification from visual candidate identification. We would examine false positives, false negatives, unsupported confidence, and performance across different lighting, image quality, language, and source types. Qualified human review and a defined evaluation protocol would be needed before making a scientific accuracy claim. The current software tests are a foundation for that work, not its replacement.

Now I will address the questions a judge is likely to ask. If the question is whether this is simply another chatbot, my answer is that the chatbot-like capability is only one optional contribution. The core artifact is the inspectable record. We preserve original media and notes, separate deterministic questions from model proposals, retain human disagreement with reasons, and export the decision history. The product remains useful when AI is turned off.

If the question is why AI is needed when local rules already exist, my answer is that the two serve different purposes. Local rules provide predictable checks for defined patterns. A language model can help identify questions in more varied phrasing, and a vision-capable model can propose visible candidate appearances. Both are constrained, and both remain subordinate to the evidence and human review. The goal is useful assistance at specific points, not replacing every rule with a model.

If the question is whether the system detects pollution from a photograph, my answer is that this version does not make that claim. It helps inspect visible appearances and retain questions. A photograph can support an observation about what appears in the frame, while pollutant identity and water safety require appropriate additional evidence. The product is designed to prevent an attractive visual output from being mistaken for a diagnosis.

If the question is whether the river illustration is a digital twin, my answer is that it is a conceptual interactive scene. It is generated geometry with perspective, lighting, and controls. It explains the workflow and gives the site a distinctive identity. A physical digital twin would require measured geometry, defined system dynamics, calibration, and validation that we have not implemented. The label beside the illustration keeps that difference visible.

If the question is whether the map shows live conditions, my answer depends on the selected layer. The Blue Marble reference is historical. The daily satellite option requests dated imagery that can contain coverage gaps. Landscape two thousand twenty-one is an annual historical composite. The weather panel contains modeled context with its own dates and retrieval information. None of these layers is silently converted into a live river sensor.

If the question is whether the dataset is synthetic, my answer is that the source photographs are genuine credited historical sources, the retained weather responses are genuine retrieved public context, and the initial presentation notes and review decisions are authored examples. Some records contain genuine retained AI responses. Those categories are labelled separately. We do not claim that the team conducted the historical visits or that the examples constitute expert validation.

If the question is why there are thirty-nine photographs but only a smaller set of saved example reviews, my answer is that a catalogue source and an assessed record are different entities. Adding a source photograph should not automatically invent a review or an AI result. The archive can grow independently, and a user can deliberately begin a credited review of a selected source. That separation is a feature of the evidence model.

If the question is how we know an AI result belongs to the selected photograph, my answer is that the system checks the reference identity and the associated media digest. Similar names or nearby places are not sufficient. The response metadata stays with the matched media. This does not prove the source scene is authentic, but it reduces accidental misassociation within the application and across retained presentation material.

If the question is whether a reviewer can disagree with AI, I will demonstrate the disposition control and reason. Support, disagreement, and uncertainty are all retained possibilities. The model proposal remains visible after a human disagrees. That gives future readers a more informative record than either automatic acceptance or deletion of the unwanted suggestion. Disagreement is a supported outcome of the workflow.

If the question is what the quality score means, my answer is evidence completeness. It describes the presence and coverage of record elements under the implemented method. It is not a water-quality index, an ecological grade, a model confidence score, or a measure of truth. A record can be well documented and still contain uncertainty. A sparse record can still contain an important observation. The score helps navigation and follow-up rather than replacing judgment.

If the question is whether hashes make the evidence tamper-proof, my answer is no. Hashes let us compare byte identity and check whether an associated file matches what was retained. They do not authenticate the author, the camera, the location, or the local decision history. Stronger authenticity guarantees would require additional systems and a defined trust model. We use hashes for the limited role they can actually support.

If the question is whether observations are stored in Google Cloud, my answer is that the public application and its server routes are hosted there, while this version's observation collection and media are stored in the browser. The optional AI request transmits the consented input through the server to the configured provider. Field packs provide deliberate transfer. Cloud hosting and cloud synchronization are different capabilities, and we have implemented the former.

If the question is whether AI really works in the deployed product, my answer is that we have recorded successful hosted text and photo requests returning validated Groq responses. I can also demonstrate a new request with appropriate consent and describe its actual result. That is stronger than a configuration badge alone. It still remains a bounded connectivity result rather than a guarantee about future service availability or scientific performance.

If the question is what happens when the model hallucinates, my answer is that we reduce the surface for unsupported output through constrained categories, exact quote grounding, schema validation, cautious application wording, and explicit human judgment. We also preserve the model's provenance and allow disagreement. These measures do not eliminate error. They make a questionable suggestion easier to identify, challenge, and keep separate from the original observation.

If the question is whether this is ready for real operational adoption, my answer is that the core workflow is implemented and deployed as a prototype, with documented software and browser checks. Broader adoption would require authenticated collaboration, operational controls, real-device testing, domain evaluation, and user research. We can point to a working foundation and a specific next validation plan. We should not claim completed field impact that we have not measured.
